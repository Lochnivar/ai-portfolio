Prometheus Limit: Stellar Exploration Simulation

An original science fiction universe where ships navigate real stellar neighborhoods using depth-dependent FTL physics, wormhole transit keys, and AI-driven exploration strategies — all built on actual astronomical data.

The Concept: Real Stars, Original Physics

"We call them Fifteeners because they're running at the Prometheus Limit. They'll tell you it's 14.95, that they've got a 50-millifathom safety margin. But when ISM gets dense, when the stellar wind shifts, when the PL surges... that margin disappears."
— Chief Engineer Rodriguez, Depth Certification Board

Prometheus Limit is an original science fiction universe built on a foundation of real astronomical data. The project imports the HYG stellar database (containing over 100,000 real stars with accurate positions and classifications) and the NASA Exoplanet Archive, then layers original FTL physics, exploration mechanics, and economic systems on top of that real-world foundation.

The core physics concept is the Prometheus Limit itself — depth 15.0 fathoms, where standard hulls fail due to tidal stress in compressed spacetime. Ships travel by "diving" into compressed space layers, trading safety margin for speed. This creates a three-tier culture: conservative Fourteeners (depth 14.0), professional Fourteen-Fivers (14.5), and elite Fifteeners (14.95) who operate a hair's breadth from hull failure.

The depth a ship operates at determines its Jordan Limit range — how far it can reach between star systems via FTL dive. At depth 14.0, the stellar neighborhood fragments into small clusters of 15 systems maximum. At 14.95, a giant 297-system network emerges. This creates fundamentally different strategic landscapes depending on risk tolerance, and drives the entire exploration simulation.

The Data Pipeline: From Catalogs to Universe

Building the simulation required a multi-phase data pipeline that transforms raw astronomical catalogs into a navigable universe with physics, economics, and mission systems:

  • Stellar Import: Ingests the HYG v41/v42 stellar database with 3D positions, spectral classifications, luminosities, and magnitudes for real stars within configurable distance limits (25 LY test neighborhood or full catalog).
  • Property Derivation: Calculates mass, radius, luminosity, Prometheus Limit boundary, and Jordan Limit range for every star based on its physical properties.
  • Exoplanet Integration: Cross-references the NASA Exoplanet Archive to attach confirmed exoplanets to their host stars, with Gaia-based matching for unresolved hosts.
  • Procedural Generation: Uses Kepler occurrence rate statistics to generate planets for stars without observed companions, creating a complete universe where every system has astrophysically plausible planetary bodies.
  • Survey & Valuation: Generates Earth-based survey data (what's known before exploration) and calculates charter values based on habitability potential, resource richness, strategic position, and distance.
  • Mission Generation: Creates four mission types — Directed Survey, Prospecting, Route Mapping, and Blind Exploration — each with unique objectives and payout structures.

The Explorer Engine: Nine Strategies, One Matrix

The heart of the simulation is the Explorer Engine — a turn-based system where ships accept missions, navigate star systems, discover wormhole transit keys, and make autonomous decisions based on their assigned strategy. Nine strategies are organized as a 3×3 matrix of mechanics (what the ship does) and risk levels (how deep it dives):

Low Risk (14.0 fa) Medium Risk (14.5 fa) High Risk (14.95 fa)
Exploration Cartographer — Exhaust all JL dives before wormhole. Dense local mapping. Scout — Prefer wormhole when safe; JL fallback. Balanced progress. Maverick — Score-based frontier push. No backtrack. Resupply at sea.
Maintenance Consolidator — Refresh keys, verify routes in known space. Surveyor — Consolidator + coffin corners. Test alternate depths. Storm Rider — Map ISM flows and PL surge zones. Extended patrol.
Pathfinding Master — Find wormhole shortcuts to replace dangerous routes. Pathfinder — Test non-standard depths to open marginal routes. Pilot — Know choke points, vulnerability windows, and refit points.

Each strategy implements a distinct behavioral tree for target selection. Exploration strategies discover new systems and create the network. Maintenance strategies verify routes and refresh transit key data in known space. Pathfinding strategies optimize the network, finding shortcuts and collapsing multi-hop routes.

Key Navigation Mechanics: Ships travel via two methods — JL dives (sailing through overlapping Jordan Limit zones between nearby stars, speed and range determined by dive depth) and wormhole jumps (near-instant transit via discovered transit keys from L6 gravitational balance points). Transit keys are one-way, discovered only when a ship arrives at a system for the first time, creating an asymmetric and growing network.

Risk isn't about environmental danger — it's about how far a ship pushes its own operational envelope. Low risk ships stay at standard depth with full safety margins and must return home on what they brought. Medium risk ships push deeper, testing tighter routes. High risk ships break the supply chain entirely, resupplying at habitable planets to extend their range indefinitely.

AI Collaboration Methodology

This project demonstrates deep AI collaboration across every phase — from universe design to physics modeling to simulation engineering:

  • Physics Design: Iterative development of the compression formula (C = exp(0.53 × d)), depth doctrine, and Prometheus Limit concept through extended human-AI dialogue. The empirical constant, safety margins, and network topology implications were refined through dozens of collaborative iterations.
  • Strategy Specification: The 3×3 strategy matrix was developed through a structured Q&A process documented in a 1,000+ line dialogue page, with AI helping formalize behavioral trees, identify edge cases (ping-pong loops, dead ends, stranding), and define precise terminology.
  • Simulation Engineering: AI-assisted implementation of the explorer engine, transit key discovery mechanics, procedural generation pipelines, and batch simulation runners with CSV/Markdown reporting.
  • Astrophysical Research: Used AI to synthesize stellar classification data, Kepler occurrence rates, and exoplanet statistics into generation algorithms that produce plausible planetary systems at scale.
  • Worldbuilding Consistency: AI served as a continuity checker across the growing canon — tracking terminology decisions (Scout vs Pathfinder naming), resolving contradictions (dark pocket vs dark cluster definitions), and maintaining a living specification document.

Key Insight: The most valuable AI contribution wasn't code generation — it was serving as a structured thinking partner for universe design. The dialogue page records 19 canonical questions with answers, 30+ defined terms, and resolved naming conflicts, all produced through iterative human-AI exchange. AI excels at holding complex systems in context and identifying where design decisions create downstream contradictions.

What the Simulation Produces

  • Ship Logs: Real-time progress tracking as ships navigate between systems, recording every dive, jump, discovery, and decision.
  • Transit Key Networks: Asymmetric wormhole connection graphs that grow as exploration progresses, creating an evolving map of navigable space.
  • Batch Simulation Reports: CSV summaries and Markdown reports comparing strategy performance across multiple runs (systems visited, keys discovered, travel days, mission payouts).
  • Decision Logs: Turn-by-turn records of why each strategy chose its target, enabling analysis of behavioral patterns and edge cases.
